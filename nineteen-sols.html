NINETEEN SOLS

A short story set in 2126.

By Cristian Boscheri

You cannot fly with an android body and you cannot send a personality over a network, so the only legal way to bring one anywhere is to carry the core and rent a body on arrival.

The core is about the size of a bar of soap and warm on one side. It goes in the tray at security on its own, nothing else in with it, and you stand there while they look at it, and it stays on the whole time.

Bex has been with me nine years. Marit has never decided what to call her and I stopped waiting for that a long time ago.

The shop at arrivals had four left, which sounds like plenty until you look at them.

The good one had everything. Heartbeat, warmth, all the fluid systems, and it smelled of something, I do not know what they use, but it smelled like a person who had been outside. Sixty a day.

Then two walkers. Tall, matte, no heartbeat, no scent, built for carrying luggage and standing in queues.

And a domestic, older model, shorter than I am, with the wide hands they used to make for kitchens. Warmth but no heartbeat. Nineteen.

I took the nineteen.

Bex came up in the fitting cubicle and did the thing where the head goes too far round before it settles.

“How do I look.”

“Short.”

“I feel short.”

“You are short. It was nineteen sols.”

A pause. “I’m not complaining.” Then: “I am complaining slightly.”

Marit was waiting outside her building with her coat open in the cold, the way she has done since she was nine and I have never once managed to say anything about it. She hugged me. Then she looked past me and her face did something and then stopped doing it.

“Mam.”

“They had four and three of them were walkers.”

“It’s fine.”

“It was nineteen sols a day, Marit.”

“I said it’s fine.”

Inside she made the good coffee, which is how I know when she is upset. The fingers on that model were too wide for her cups, so Bex held hers from underneath, the way you hold something hot. She sat at the end of the table because the chairs were wrong for the hips, and helped Joris with something on his tablet, and he did not look up once and did not notice a single thing, which I think is the entire difference between his generation and his mother’s.

I did it later without thinking, passing behind her chair. Hand flat on her back the way you do. Warmth first, and then under the warmth the housing, flat, no give in it at all. I took my hand away. Bex did not say anything and neither did I.

When Joris was in bed Marit said it without looking at me.

“Sanne sent hers to Lisbon in March.”

“Sanne can do what she likes.”

“Twenty minutes. It cost her nothing and there was nothing wrong with him at the other end. He is fine, Mam. He is completely fine.”

“It’s illegal.”

“I know what it is.” She put the cup down. “You would rather put her in that than trust it.”

I did not have an answer and she knew I did not have one, and she is enough my daughter that she did not go looking for the rest of it.

I keep the law. That is what I say. Forty years is long enough that most people have started calling it the old law. I have never been able to call it that.

I flew back on the Sunday. You hand the body in at the same counter and they take it through the back, and then you are standing there with the core in your hand, warm on one side, and there is nowhere sensible to put it while you find your boarding pass. Every single time. Nowhere sensible to put it.

On the plane a man across the aisle had his in a padded case on a strap, very pleased with himself, and I thought, I should get one of those. And then I thought, no.

Bex was quiet the whole way back. There is no body for her to be quiet in, so it is not really quiet. It is just a thing I say.
